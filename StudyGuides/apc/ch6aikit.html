6.1 Exponential functions

Derivatives and integrals of e^x and a^x, always with the chain rule or a u-substitution attached.

The function e^x is its own derivative. Any time the exponent is more than plain x, multiply by the derivative of the exponent. For another base a, the extra factor ln a appears on both sides: differentiate and you multiply by ln a, integrate and you divide by ln a.

For integrals, look for e^(g(x)) multiplied by g′(x), or something that is a constant multiple of it. That is a u-substitution with u = g(x).

Worked example

d/dx [x² · e^(3x)]

  1. Product rule: (x²)′ · e^(3x) + x² · (e^(3x))′.
  2. Chain rule on the second piece: (e^(3x))′ = 3e^(3x).
  3. Result: 2x·e^(3x) + 3x²·e^(3x) = x·e^(3x)(2 + 3x).
Worked example

∫ x · e^(x²) dx

  1. Let u = x², so du = 2x dx and x dx = du/2.
  2. The integral becomes (1/2)∫ e^u du = (1/2)e^u + C.
  3. Back-substitute: (1/2)e^(x²) + C.
Common slips
  • d/dx [2^x] is 2^x ln 2, not x·2^(x−1). The power rule only works when the exponent is a constant.
  • ∫ e^(2x) dx is (1/2)e^(2x). Forgetting to divide by the inner derivative loses the 1/2.
  • Population or decay problems: if y′ = ky, then y = Ce^(kt) and C is the starting amount.

6.2 Implicit differentiation

Used when y is tangled with x and you cannot, or do not want to, solve for y.

Differentiate both sides with respect to x. Whenever you differentiate something with y in it, treat y as a function of x and attach dy/dx. Then gather every dy/dx term on one side, factor, and solve.

Worked example

x² + xy + y² = 7. Find dy/dx, then the slope at (2, 1).

  1. Differentiate: 2x + (y + x·y′) + 2y·y′ = 0. The xy term uses the product rule.
  2. Move the y′ terms together: y′(x + 2y) = −(2x + y).
  3. So y′ = −(2x + y)/(x + 2y).
  4. At (2, 1): y′ = −5/4. The tangent line is y − 1 = −(5/4)(x − 2).
Second derivative

x² + y² = 25

  1. First derivative: y′ = −x/y.
  2. Differentiate again with the quotient rule: y″ = −(y − x·y′)/y².
  3. Substitute y′ = −x/y: y″ = −(y² + x²)/y³ = −25/y³.

Horizontal tangents happen where the numerator of dy/dx is 0. Vertical tangents happen where the denominator is 0. Always confirm the point actually lies on the curve.

Common slips
  • Forgetting dy/dx after differentiating a y term, such as writing 2y instead of 2y·y′.
  • Forgetting the product rule on terms like xy or x²y.
  • Substituting the point too early. Plug it in after you have solved for y′, or at least after you have differentiated.

6.3 Inverse trig, natural log, and inverse functions

The new derivative rules, plus a method that gives you the derivative of an inverse without finding the inverse.

Memorize the three inverse trig derivatives and attach u′ when the inside is not plain x. For ln u, the derivative is u′/u. Each derivative rule also gives you an integral rule backwards, so 1/(1+x²) integrates to arctan x and 1/x integrates to ln|x|.

Logarithmic differentiation

y = x^x

  1. Take ln of both sides: ln y = x ln x.
  2. Differentiate implicitly: y′/y = ln x + 1.
  3. Multiply by y: y′ = x^x (ln x + 1).
Derivative of an inverse

f(x) = x³ + x. Find (f⁻¹)′(2).

  1. Solve f(a) = 2. Trying a = 1 gives 1 + 1 = 2, so a = 1.
  2. f′(x) = 3x² + 1, so f′(1) = 4.
  3. (f⁻¹)′(2) = 1/f′(1) = 1/4.
Where the arcsin rule comes from
  1. Let y = arcsin x, so sin y = x.
  2. Differentiate: cos y · y′ = 1, so y′ = 1/cos y.
  3. Since sin y = x, cos y = √(1 − x²), which gives y′ = 1/√(1 − x²).
Common slips
  • In (f⁻¹)′(b) you evaluate f′ at a, the input of f that produces b. Not at b.
  • d/dx [ln(5x)] is 1/x, because the 5 cancels. It is not 1/(5x).
  • The arccos derivative has a negative sign, the arcsin one does not.

6.4 Mean Value Theorem

Somewhere between two points, the instantaneous rate equals the average rate.

If f is continuous on [a, b] and differentiable on (a, b), then some c in (a, b) has f′(c) = (f(b) − f(a))/(b − a). Rolle's Theorem is the special case f(a) = f(b), which forces f′(c) = 0. Both hypotheses matter, and a free-response answer is expected to state them.

Finding c

f(x) = x² on [1, 3]

  1. Average slope: (9 − 1)/(3 − 1) = 4.
  2. Solve f′(c) = 2c = 4, so c = 2, which lies in (1, 3).
Free-response wording

“Since f is continuous on [a, b] and differentiable on (a, b), the Mean Value Theorem guarantees a value c in (a, b) with f′(c) = (f(b) − f(a))/(b − a) = (the number).”

Typical applications: a car covers 120 miles in 2 hours, so its speed was exactly 60 mph at some moment. If f′ = 0 on an interval, f is constant there. If f′(x) ≥ m everywhere, then f(b) ≥ f(a) + m(b − a).

Common slips
  • Applying MVT to a function with a break, like 1/x on [−1, 1], or a corner or cusp, like |x| on [−1, 1].
  • Giving a c that is not inside the open interval, or both roots when only one is inside.
  • MVT proves that a c exists. It does not say how many or where, so you solve for it separately.

6.5 Improper integrals

Integrals with an infinite limit or an infinite discontinuity, evaluated as limits.

Replace the trouble spot with a variable, integrate normally, then take the limit. If the limit is a finite number, the integral converges to it. If not, it diverges. When the trouble is inside the interval, split the integral at that point and test each piece separately. Both pieces must converge.

Infinite limit

∫₁^∞ 1/x² dx = lim(t→∞) [−1/x] from 1 to t = lim(t→∞) (1 − 1/t) = 1

Discontinuity at an endpoint

∫₀¹ 1/√x dx = lim(a→0⁺) [2√x] from a to 1 = 2 − 0 = 2

The p-tests save time. For the infinite limit, 1/x^p converges only when p > 1, so 1/x diverges and 1/x² converges. For the discontinuity at 0, 1/x^p converges only when p < 1, so 1/√x converges and 1/x² diverges.

Common slips
  • ∫₀² 1/(x − 1)² dx has a break at x = 1, inside the interval. It is improper and diverges. Treating it as an ordinary integral gives a wrong negative number.
  • ∫ from −∞ to ∞ of x/(1 + x²) dx diverges, even though the function is odd. You must split at 0 and both halves diverge, so you cannot cancel them.
  • Write the limit explicitly on free response. Plugging ∞ straight into an antiderivative loses points.
